Periodisation Is a Hypothesis — Data Is How You Test It

Every training block is a bet.

You predict that a certain stimulus — volume, intensity, frequency, modality — applied over a certain duration, will produce a certain adaptation. More endurance. More strength. A faster race time. A lower injury rate. You write the programme, the athlete executes it, and then... most coaches move on to the next block without ever auditing whether the bet paid off.

That's a problem. Not because coaches are lazy — they're not. It's because closing the loop is genuinely hard when you don't have the data infrastructure to do it.

What "closing the loop" actually means

Closing the loop means comparing the training stimulus you prescribed against the adaptation you actually saw. Did the athlete's threshold power go up after 8 weeks of sweet-spot work? Did the strength block translate to better force application in the field? Did the deload week actually reduce the fatigue indicators, or did the athlete train through it anyway?

To answer these questions you need two things: accurate records of what was prescribed and what was actually done, and reliable markers of adaptation over time.

Most coaches have a loose version of the first. Very few have a systematic approach to the second.

The problem with intuition-only coaching

Experienced coaches develop strong intuitions. They can read an athlete's fatigue in their posture before the athlete says a word. That's real, and it's valuable. But intuition is a pattern-matching system, and it can only match patterns it has seen before.

An athlete who's quietly overtrained looks a lot like an athlete who's just undertapered in the week before a peak. An athlete who's adapting well to a new stimulus can feel flat for two to three weeks before the gains show up. Without data, these are hard to distinguish. With data, they're often obvious in retrospect — and occasionally predictable in advance.

Intuition is where you start. Data is how you get better.

What good data practice looks like

You don't need to turn every training session into a science experiment. You need a few reliable signals, tracked consistently over time:

  • Training load — both prescribed and actual
  • A subjective wellness marker — sleep, mood, perceived recovery
  • A periodic objective performance marker — a test set, a race result, a measured benchmark

Plot these over time. Look for divergence. When the training load is high and the wellness markers hold steady, the athlete is adapting. When the load is moderate and the wellness markers are tanking, something is wrong. You probably knew that already — but now you have a timestamp to point to.

That timestamp is what lets you refine the hypothesis next time.
